# Our findings and their human neuroeconomic counterparts (for the slides)

**This is an analogy, not a test.** We measured no neural or physiological data. Each row places a behavioural LLM
finding next to the human neuroeconomic result it resembles, says how close the parallel is, and names the difference.
All references are in `docs/literature_review.md` (bibliographic details checked 13 Nov 2026).

| # | LLM finding (our evidence, strength) | Human neuroeconomic counterpart | How close the analogy is |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **A graded "value signal" exists and drives choice.** The models' P(accept) from log-probabilities matches their sampled choices (all 7 runs; `results/value_signal/`). In the large middle bins the accept rate is within about 2 points of the mean P(accept). Choices are more extreme than P at the ends (0% accepted where P ≈ .07, 100% where P ≈ .95, probably because the sampler truncates unlikely tokens), and sparse bins (50–100 trials) deviate by up to 17 points. **Strong.** | Neural value signals (ventral striatum, vmPFC) scale with the value of an option and predict choice; formal models link a latent value to noisy choice (Rilling & Sanfey, 2011; Xiang, Lohrenz & Montague, 2013). | **Formal parallel.** Both are "latent value → stochastic choice". Ours is an output probability, not a neural code, and it is not localised anywhere in the network. |
| 2 | **Fairness raises acceptance in Qwen, not in Llama.** Qwen's P(accept) rises with the offer; Llama's curve is almost flat, although it answers the payoff questions 100% correctly (extension 2). **Strong.** | Fair offers engage reward circuitry beyond their money value (Tabibnia, Satpute & Lieberman, 2008). Disrupting right DLPFC makes people accept unfair offers **while still judging them unfair** (Knoch et al., 2006). | **Close conceptual parallel to Knoch et al.:** Llama *knows* the payoffs but does not *enforce* fairness, a dissociation between understanding and acting on a norm. No claim about any mechanism. |
| 3 | **A counterpart's anger makes an offer less acceptable.** The value shift for anger is negative in 7 of 7 runs (Qwen −1.1 to −2.2 logit, Llama −0.07 to −0.46). Qwen-7B's indifference offer rises from about 22% to 51% of the pie (+29 pp [6, 57]; Q8 +34 pp [4, 71]). None survives Holm across the 21 tests. **Moderate** (consistent direction; runs share messages, so they are not independent). | Rejection of unfair offers is driven by negative emotion: anterior insula activity predicts rejection (Sanfey et al., 2003), and the responder's anger, not a fairness judgement alone, predicts rejection (Pillutla & Murnighan, 1996). | **Partial.** In humans it is the responder's **own** anger; here it is the **counterpart's** anger, shown in a message. The LLM behaves as if anger raises its minimum acceptable offer, a "tougher responder" pattern. |
| 4 | **Anger lowers trust.** Anger reduces the amount sent in the trust game: replicated in 2 pre-registered studies, 4 Qwen variants and Llama (−10 to −22 pp). **Strong** (but message-dependent and absent in Mistral). | Expressed anger is read as social information about the sender's intentions and limits (van Kleef, De Dreu & Manstead, 2004; Sinaceur & Tiedens, 2006). Trust decisions track expected reciprocity: caudate signals follow a partner's reputation (King-Casas et al., 2005), and oxytocin raises transfers (Kosfeld et al., 2005). | **Functional parallel.** The models treat anger as a cue that the partner is less likely to reciprocate, which is what the emotion-as-information account predicts. We do not know whether people would cut trust by the same amount; our human ratings asked about giving, not trust. |
| 5 | **The sadness effect was really the described loss.** Qwen rejected less after sad messages that described a bereavement, but not after event-free sad messages (F1 not replicated; CI excludes the main estimate). Qwen-14B's value curve after sad messages is high and flat (+4.08 logit; slope −7.0; both Holm p = .003). **Moderate.** | Incidental vs integral emotion: emotions carried over from an unrelated event change economic valuation (Lerner, Small & Loewenstein, 2004), and incidental sadness changes UG rejection (Harlé & Sanfey, 2007). | **Partial, and instructive.** The human studies manipulate the decider's **own** mood; ours manipulates the **counterpart's** state. The result mirrors the integral/incidental distinction: the model responded to the *situation* (a loss), not to the *emotion label*. |
| 6 | **Deliberation goes with generosity.** Mistral, the only reasoning model, gives and returns more after emotional messages, and its reasoning text mentions empathy in 75–96% of sad trials (keyword coding). **Weak** (one model; reasoning text may be post-hoc rationalisation). | Dual-process accounts of the UG: rejection as a contest between an emotional reaction (insula) and a cognitive goal (DLPFC) (Sanfey et al., 2003; Knoch et al., 2006). | **Loose.** "Reasoning" in an LLM is generated text, not a separable control system. Present it only as an interesting contrast. |
| 7 | **Recognising ≠ acting.** Emotion recognition is 98–100%, yet most emotion effects are equivalent to zero (Qwen-7B: 11 of 15). **Strong.** | Knowing a norm and enforcing it rely on dissociable processes (Knoch et al., 2006); emotion recognition and social decision-making are distinct functions. | **Conceptual parallel.** The models perceive the emotion, but it mostly does not enter the decision. |

## What we can and cannot say
- **Can:** the models show behaviour that *functionally resembles* several human neuroeconomic findings. Anger raises the bar
  for accepting and lowers trust; a described loss raises acceptance and generosity; a latent value signal drives choice;
  knowing the rules is separate from using fairness.
- **Cannot:** that any of this happens through the same mechanism as in the brain, that the models "feel" anything, or that
  a model component corresponds to the insula, striatum or DLPFC. We tested no internal representations.
- **One-line framing for the slides:** *LLMs as subjects in a neuroeconomics paradigm: a behavioural test of whether
  emotional signals enter the decision the way neuroeconomic theory says they do in humans.*
